title: "Why Second Brains Fail"
source_type: note
captured: 2026-08-12
---

Rough notes to myself after abandoning my fourth note-taking system.

Every system I've quit died the same way. I saved things faster than I understood them.
The app filled up, the folders multiplied, and at some point the pile stopped being a
resource and started being a debt. There's a name for this — the collector's fallacy —
the quiet belief that having saved something is the same as having learned it. It isn't.
Saving is cheap and takes a second; understanding is expensive and takes attention. A
system that makes saving frictionless and understanding optional will always drift toward
a landfill.

What seems to actually work is the opposite bet: make the system refuse to store anything
it hasn't digested. One idea per note, written in your own words, titled as a claim you
could defend. That's the Zettelkasten discipline — atomic notes. The constraint sounds
fussy until you notice what it buys you: a note about exactly one thing can be linked to
from anywhere, reused in any argument, and revised without collateral damage. A note about
six things can't be linked to precisely, so it never gets linked at all, so it may as well
not exist.

The other half is that the value isn't in the notes, it's in the links between them. An
unlinked note is a dead end you'll never find again. A linked note is reachable from every
neighbour it touches, and it gets more reachable every time you add something nearby. The
collection compounds, which a folder hierarchy never does — folders force one location per
idea, and every idea worth keeping belongs in several places at once.

So the test for any system: does it get *more* useful as it gets bigger, or less? Most get
less. That's the whole problem.
