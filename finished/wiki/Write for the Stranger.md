# Write for the Stranger

Whatever you write down, you are writing it for someone who wasn't there. In eight months
that someone is you: the meeting, the context, the argument it was responding to, and the
reason you cared will all be gone. A note that only makes sense to a reader who already
remembers the thing it's about is doing no work.

## Why it's invisible at the time

At the moment of writing you're holding all the context in your head, so four words and a
squiggle feel completely clear. The gap only opens later, which is why nobody catches it as
it happens — the failure has no symptoms until it's too late to fix. Old notebooks full of
gibberish that was once obvious are the usual evidence.

## The test

Could someone who wasn't there understand this? Run it against the version of you that has
forgotten everything. A note that fails is a souvenir — a token that something happened —
which is fine to keep, as long as you don't mistake it for a note.

## What it costs

Less than expected. Passing the test doesn't mean explaining the whole source; it means
carrying one idea plus enough scaffolding for it to stand on its own, which is usually a
sentence of context. The goal is to make a single idea survive without you — not to
reproduce what you read.

That constraint is the same one [[Atomic Notes]] arrives at from the other direction: one
idea, stated so it holds up alone. It's also what makes [[Linked Thought]] possible, since a
note that can't be understood on its own can't usefully be linked to from anywhere else.
[[Progressive Summarization]] describes when to do this writing — in the last pass, not the
first.
